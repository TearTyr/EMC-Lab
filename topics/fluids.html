Fluids

Fluids are liquids and gases. This topic explains why pressure grows with depth, why things float or sink, and how moving fluid can lift an aeroplane.

A fluid — liquid or gas — cannot resist being reshaped, so it pushes equally in every direction and its pressure grows with depth. From those two facts fall everything else: why ships float, how hydraulic presses lift cars, and how a wing makes lift out of moving air.

Learning objectives

  • Define density and pressure, and compute pressure at depth with P = P₀ + ρgh.
  • State Archimedes’ principle and predict floating depth from the density ratio.
  • Explain sinking, floating and apparent weight in terms of forces.
  • Apply the continuity equation A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ to a narrowing pipe.
  • Use Bernoulli’s equation qualitatively: faster flow, lower pressure — on wings and in venturis.

2.1 · Density and pressure

Density is how tightly mass is packed; pressure is how hard a fluid pushes. Go deeper and the push grows, because more fluid weighs on you.

Density ρ — mass per unit volume
ρ = m/V, in kg/m³. Water: 1000 kg/m³ (a litre weighs a kilogram); air at sea level: about 1.2 kg/m³; steel: 7800 kg/m³.
Pressure P — force per unit area
P = F/A, in pascals (Pa = N/m²). One atmosphere ≈ 101 kPa — the column of air above you presses with the weight of about a tonne on every square metre, and you do not feel it because your body pushes back equally.
P = P₀ + ρ·g·h P₀ = pressure at the surface (usually 1 atm) · h = depth below it · ρ = fluid density

Pressure in a still fluid grows linearly with depth: every layer of fluid must support the weight of everything above it. Direction does not matter — pressure at a point pushes equally in all directions (Pascal), which is why a small force on a small piston becomes a big force on a big piston: F₂ = F₁·A₂/A₁, the hydraulic press.

Worked example

What is the absolute pressure 10 m below a lake surface? (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s², P₀ = 101 kPa)

P = 101 000 + 1000 × 9.81 × 10 = 101 000 + 98 100 = 199 kPa ≈ 2 atm — every 10 m of water adds roughly one atmosphere.

2.2 · Archimedes’ principle

Anything in a fluid gets pushed up by the weight of the fluid it shoves aside. That is why less-dense things float and denser things sink.

FB = ρfluid · Vdisplaced · g the buoyant force equals the WEIGHT of the fluid pushed aside

Why does this force exist? Pressure grows with depth, so the fluid pushes up on the bottom face of a submerged body harder than it pushes down on the top face. The difference is exactly the weight of the displaced fluid — that is Archimedes’ principle, and it yields the floating rule:

  • Floats (ρobj < ρfluid): the body settles until displaced weight = its own weight. Submerged fraction = ρobj/ρfluid — ice shows 8% of itself above the surface because 917/1000 ≈ 0.92 is below.
  • Sinks (ρobj > ρfluid): even fully submerged, FB < W. The floor supplies the rest as a normal force — the “apparent weight” underwater is W − FB.
Real world — steel ships and load lines

A cargo ship is steel, eight times denser than water — but it is a steel shell around air. What floats is the ship’s average density. Load the hold until the average density passes water’s and the deck goes under; that is why hulls carry Plimsoll load lines, one mark per water type: a ship floats deeper in warm fresh water (ρ ≈ 997) than in cold seawater (ρ ≈ 1025).

Common misconceptions

1. “Heavier things sink.” A 100-tonne ship floats; a 5-gram steel bolt sinks. What matters is density — mass per volume — not mass.

2. “Buoyancy increases with depth.” For a fully submerged, incompressible body it is constant: FB depends on displaced volume, and that does not grow as the body descends.

3. “Floating means no forces act.” Floating is equilibrium: weight down, buoyancy up, equal magnitudes. The net force is zero because two real forces balance, not because forces stopped existing.

2.4 · Moving fluids: continuity and Bernoulli

When a fluid speeds up, its pressure drops. That single idea explains wing lift, narrow-pipe suction and many everyday surprises.

Set a fluid in motion and two new rules appear. First, continuity: fluid is not created or destroyed, so whatever flows into a pipe must flow out — in a narrower section it must move faster:

A₁·v₁ = A₂·v₂ narrower pipe ⇒ faster flow — the same litres per second, squeezed through less area

Second, Bernoulli: for steady, incompressible, frictionless flow along a streamline, the sum of pressure, kinetic and gravitational energy density is constant:

P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant the headline: where the fluid speeds up, its pressure DROPS

That counter-intuitive sentence — faster flow, lower pressure — powers most of the fluid world: a wing’s curved top surface runs air faster than the bottom, so the pressure above is lower and the pressure difference lifts the aeroplane; a venturi’s throat sucks fuel into a carburettor; wind over your roof lowers the pressure above it, which is why hurricanes lift roofs off rather than push them down.

The “equal transit time” myth

Textbooks once claimed air splits at the leading edge and the two halves meet again at the trailing edge, forcing the longer top path to be faster. They do not meet. In real flow the air over the top arrives well ahead of the air underneath — it is faster still than the myth suggests. Lift comes from the wing deflecting air downward (Newton III) and from the pressure difference that goes with it (Bernoulli) — the two explanations are the same physics viewed two ways, and neither needs equal transit times.

2.6 · Check your understanding

A 1200 kg car rests on a hydraulic lift. The car piston has 100× the area of the input piston. What force must you apply?
Pressure is transmitted unchanged (Pascal), so F scales with area: F = 1200·g/100 ≈ 118 N — about the push of a 12 kg weight. You pay for it in distance: your piston travels 100× farther.
An object floats in water with 30% of its volume above the surface. What is its density?
70% submerged ⇒ ρobj = 0.70 × 1000 = 700 kg/m³ — roughly oak or a very full rubbish bin.
A 2 kg block (density 2500 kg/m³) hangs from a string, fully submerged in water. What does the string feel?
V = m/ρ = 2/2500 = 0.0008 m³ → FB = 1000 × 0.0008 × 9.81 = 7.85 N. Tension = W − FB = 19.6 − 7.85 = 11.8 N — it feels about 40% lighter underwater.
Water flows at 1 m/s through a pipe whose diameter halves at a junction. How fast is it in the narrow section?
Area scales with diameter², so A₂ = A₁/4 and v₂ = 4·v₁ = 4 m/s. Halving the diameter quadruples the speed — continuity bites hard.
Why do two ships sailing close side-by-side risk being pulled together?
Water squeezed between the hulls speeds up, and by Bernoulli its pressure drops below the pressure on the outer sides — the higher outside pressure pushes the ships together. The same effect slams a shower curtain inward while the water runs.
