Measurement & vectors

Physics starts with measuring things. This topic teaches two tools you will use everywhere: checking units, and adding arrows (vectors) the correct way.

Physics is a science of measured numbers — and every number carries a unit. Before any of the later topics make sense, you need two tools: dimensional analysis, which catches algebra mistakes before they happen, and vectors, which let a single arrow carry both a size and a direction.

Learning objectives

  • Name the SI base units and convert between prefixes (k, m, µ, n).
  • Check any equation with dimensional analysis and derive units from definitions.
  • Distinguish scalars from vectors and resolve a vector into components.
  • Add vectors graphically (tip-to-tail, parallelogram) and by components.
  • Compute the magnitude and direction of a resultant, and place it in the right quadrant.

1.1 · Units and the SI system

Every measurement is a number with a unit, like 5 kg. Using the same standard units everywhere is what stops costly mix-ups.

Every measurement in physics is a number times a unit — and the number is meaningless without it. The Système International (SI) defines seven base units; everything else is built from them.

Derived units are products of base units with their own names: the newton (N = kg·m/s², force), the joule (J = N·m, energy), the watt (W = J/s, power), the pascal (Pa = N/m², pressure) — you will meet all four in the topics that follow. Prefixes scale a unit by powers of ten: kilo (k, 10³), centi (c, 10⁻²), milli (m, 10⁻³), micro (µ, 10⁻⁶), nano (n, 10⁻⁹). One rule saves endless confusion: a prefix belongs to its unit — 1 km/s is 1000 m/s, never “kilo (m/s)”.

Real world — the $327 million unit error

In 1999 NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter because one engineering team reported thruster impulses in pound-force seconds while the navigation software expected newton-seconds. The spacecraft approached Mars along the wrong trajectory and burned up. Unit bookkeeping is not pedantry — it is a safety system.

1.2 · Dimensional analysis

A quick sanity check for any formula: both sides must use the same kind of units. If they don't, the formula is wrong — no calculation needed.

Every physical quantity has dimensions built from mass [M], length [L] and time [T]. Velocity is length per time, [L][T]−1; acceleration is [L][T]−2; force is [M][L][T]−2. Two rules do all the work:

  • You may only add or compare quantities with the same dimensions — “3 m + 5 s” is not a thing.
  • Both sides of a valid equation have the same dimensions, and arguments of sin, exp, log… are dimensionless.
Worked example — check an equation before you trust it

Is v² = u² + 2as dimensionally consistent?

[v²] = [L]²[T]−2  ·  [u²] = [L]²[T]−2 ✓  ·  [2as] = [L][T]−2 × [L] = [L]²[T]−2 ✓ — all three terms match, so the equation could be right. (It is — that is the constant-acceleration formula.)

Dimensional analysis cannot catch a wrong pure number (a missing ½), but it catches every wrong shape instantly.

1.3 · Vectors and components

Some quantities (like force or velocity) have a direction as well as a size, so we draw them as arrows. To add arrows, split each into horizontal and vertical parts, add the parts, then recombine.

A scalar has magnitude only (mass, temperature, time, energy). A vector has magnitude and direction (displacement, velocity, force, acceleration) and obeys vector addition. Walking 3 km east then 4 km north does not put you 7 km away — it puts you 5 km away, because displacements add as arrows, not as numbers.

Components: the whole game

Ax = A·cos θ  ·  Ay = A·sin θ |A| = √(Ax² + Ay²)  ·  θ = tan⁻¹(Ay/Ax) θ measured from the +x axis; signs of Ax, Ay fix the quadrant

Adding vectors then becomes ordinary arithmetic per direction: Rx = Ax + Bx and Ry = Ay + By. Subtraction is addition of the negative: −B is B flipped 180°. Graphically the same answer appears as the tip-to-tail walk (put B’s tail on A’s tip; R closes the trip) or the parallelogram diagonal — both are drawn live in the simulator below.

Worked example

A hiker walks 3 km east, then 4 km north. How far is she from the start, and in what direction?

Rx = 3, Ry = 4 → |R| = √(9+16) = 5 km, θ = tan⁻¹(4/3) = 53.1° north of east.

Common misconceptions

1. “|A + B| = |A| + |B|.” Only when the vectors point the same way. In general the resultant lies between ||A| − |B|| and |A| + |B| — the simulator’s “cancel to zero” preset shows the extreme.

2. “tan⁻¹ gives the direction.” It gives an angle, but the calculator cannot know your quadrant: A = (−3, +4) and A = (3, −4) have the same tangent. Always finish with the signs — sketch the arrow.

3. “A negative vector points down.” Negative applies to a component, relative to your chosen axes. Choose axes along the slope and “negative x” means downhill along that slope.

1.5 · Check your understanding

Express the joule in SI base units.
J = N·m = (kg·m/s²)·m = kg·m²/s² = kg·m²·s−2. (The watt is one step further: W = J/s = kg·m²·s−3.)
A vector of magnitude 10 points 30° above the +x axis. Find its components.
Ax = 10·cos 30° = 8.66, Ay = 10·sin 30° = 5.00. Check: √(8.66² + 5²) = √100 = 10 ✓.
Two forces of 5 N and 12 N act on the same point at right angles. What is the resultant?
√(25 + 144) = 13 N, at tan⁻¹(12/5) = 67.4° from the 5 N force — the 5-12-13 Pythagorean triple.
Is the equation T = 2π√(L/g) for a pendulum’s period dimensionally consistent?
Yes. [L/g] = [L]/([L][T]−2) = [T]², so √(L/g) has dimensions [T], and 2π is dimensionless. (It is also physically correct — that is the real pendulum formula.)
You walk 6 km north, then 6 km south-east (45° below +x). Are you back where you started?
No. The second leg has components (6·cos45°, −6·sin45°) = (4.24, −4.24) km. Total displacement = (4.24, 6 − 4.24) = (4.24, 1.76) km — about 4.6 km away, 22.5° north of east. Only an exactly opposite vector cancels.
