Temperature & heat

Temperature says how fast the particles of a body are jittering on average; heat is energy on the move from hot to cold. Keeping those two apart — and knowing how energy travels — explains everything from sea breezes to thermos flasks.

Learning objectives

  • Convert confidently between °C, °F and K.
  • Distinguish temperature, heat and internal energy.
  • Solve specific-heat problems with Q = mcΔT, including phase changes.
  • Identify conduction, convection and radiation in real situations.
  • Explain why metals feel cold, why radiators sit under windows, and how a vacuum flask works.

3.1 · Temperature and the three scales

A thermometer works by thermal equilibrium: put it in contact with something and wait, and the reading stops moving when both share the same temperature (the zeroth law of thermodynamics). Three scales describe that number:

°F = °C × 9/5 + 32  ·  K = °C + 273.15 and the reverse: °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9 · °C = K − 273.15

Two habits prevent every conversion error: subtract the 32 before rescaling (it is an offset between zero points), and remember that a kelvin and a Celsius degree are the same size — only their zeroes differ. That is why temperature differences never need converting: ΔT = 10 °C = 10 K (though = 18 °F). Physics equations always want kelvin, because only kelvin starts at true zero motion.

3.3 · Heat and specific heat capacity

Heat Q is energy transferred because of a temperature difference — it flows spontaneously from hot to cold and is measured in joules. How much a body’s temperature rises per joule depends on its mass and its material:

Q = m·c·ΔT m in kg · c = specific heat capacity in J/(kg·K) · ΔT in K (or °C — the step size is identical)
Specific heat capacities you should recognise
Materialc, J/(kg·K)What it means
water (liquid)4186huge — oceans store summer heat for winter
ice2100half of liquid water
aluminium900heats fast — good for pans
iron / steel450a frying pan handle’s problem
copper385even faster to heat

During a phase change the temperature stands still: every joule goes into breaking bonds instead of speeding particles up. Water needs Lf = 334 kJ/kg to melt and a remarkable Lv = 2260 kJ/kg to boil — five times more energy to vaporise water at 100 °C than to heat it from 20 to 100 °C. That is why steam burns so badly: it dumps its latent heat into your skin before it even cools down.

Worked example — the kettle

How much energy to boil 1 kg of water from 20 °C? And how long in a 2.2 kW kettle?

Q = mcΔT = 1 × 4186 × 80 = 335 kJ  ·  t = Q/P = 335 000/2200 ≈ 152 s ≈ 2½ min (real kettles take a little longer — losses).

3.4 · The three ways heat moves

Conduction — contact, particle to particle
Vibrating atoms and (in metals) free electrons pass energy along a material without the material moving. Rate ∝ k·A·ΔT/L: k is the thermal conductivity — copper 401 W/(m·K), glass 1.0, wood 0.15, air 0.026. Still air is an insulator; that is what fleeces, double glazing and loft wool are really trapping.
Convection — the fluid itself travels
Heated fluid expands, becomes less dense and rises; cooler fluid sinks to replace it, setting up a circulation loop. Convection needs a fluid and gravity: radiators sit under windows to intercept sinking cold air, and there is no convection aboard the ISS.
Radiation — electromagnetic waves
Every body radiates; output grows as T⁴ (Stefan–Boltzmann, P = εσAT⁴), which is why a small temperature rise means much more radiation. It needs no medium at all — sunlight crosses 150 million km of vacuum. Matt black surfaces absorb and emit best (ε ≈ 0.95); shiny surfaces reflect (ε ≈ 0.05).
Common misconceptions

1. “Heat rises.” Heat is not a substance. Hot air rises because it is less dense — that is convection. In a vacuum, nothing rises; radiation still travels in straight lines.

2. “Metal feels cold, so metal is colder.” On a table indoors, the metal and the wood are at exactly the same temperature — your skin is the thermometer, and metal conducts your heat away faster.

3. “A coat warms you up.” A coat generates nothing; it slows the loss of your heat. Wrap one around an ice cube and the ice melts more slowly.

4. “ΔT must be converted to kelvin before using Q = mcΔT.” No — a change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K, so differences pass through untouched. Only absolute temperatures (as in gas laws or T⁴) need kelvin.

3.6 · Check your understanding

Convert −196 °C (liquid nitrogen) to °F and K.
K = −196 + 273.15 = 77.15 K. °F = −196 × 9/5 + 32 = −352.8 + 32 = −320.8 °F.
How much heat raises 0.5 kg of aluminium from 20 °C to 180 °C? (c = 900 J/kg·K)
Q = mcΔT = 0.5 × 900 × 160 = 72 kJ. The same change in water would need 0.5 × 4186 × 160 = 335 kJ — nearly five times more, which is why pans are metal and their contents are wet.
A 0.5 kW heater radiates into a room. If you triple your distance from it, how much intensity reaches you?
Intensity follows the inverse square law: I = P/(4πd²). Tripling d divides I by 9 — you feel one ninth of the warmth.
Name the dominant heat-transfer mechanism: (a) a spoon handle warming in soup, (b) a fridge coil cooling air at the top of the compartment, (c) feeling a fire on your face from 3 m away.
(a) Conduction through the metal. (b) Convection — cooled air is denser and sinks, setting up the loop. (c) Radiation — the air between you and the fire is barely warmed; infrared crosses the gap (and would cross a vacuum).
Why does a vacuum flask keep coffee hot for hours?
It attacks all three mechanisms at once: the vacuum between the walls kills conduction and convection (no particles to carry energy), the silvered walls reflect radiation back (low emissivity), and the stopper blocks convection and evaporation at the only remaining opening.
