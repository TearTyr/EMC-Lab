Magnetism & Induction

Magnetic fields arise from moving charge and from the intrinsic spin of particles such as electrons. A changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces a voltage. The two simulations below show both halves of that story.

Magnetism arises from moving charge and from the intrinsic spin of particles such as electrons. The magnetic force on a moving charge is always perpendicular to its velocity — it changes the charge’s direction but does no work — and a changing flux of a field whose lines form closed loops through a coil becomes a voltage. This chapter joins the two halves: the Lorentz force, then Faraday’s and Lenz’s laws.

Learning objectives

  • Describe magnetic field lines and why monopoles are absent.
  • Apply F = qv × B and predict orbit radius r = mv/(|q|B) and period T = 2πm/(|q|B).
  • Compute magnetic flux Φ = BA·cosθ and explain why only change induces.
  • Use EMF = −N·dΦ/dt and Lenz’s law to get magnitudes and directions.
  • Read the simulated Φ(t) and EMF(t) traces against the theory.

3.1 · Where magnetism comes from

Every magnetic field in the universe arises from moving electric charge or from the intrinsic spin of particles such as electrons — a current in a wire, or the electrons inside atoms. In a permanent magnet, huge numbers of atomic magnetic moments line up in regions called domains. Heating or hammering a magnet randomises the domains and destroys it; that is why there is a Curie temperature above which ferromagnetism vanishes (1043 K for iron).

Magnetic poles
Every magnet has a north-seeking (N) and a south-seeking (S) pole. Like poles repel, unlike poles attract — the same rule as charges.
No monopoles
Cut a bar magnet in half and you get two smaller magnets, each with its own N and S. No experiment has ever isolated a single magnetic pole, so magnetic field lines have no beginning and no end.
Magnetic flux density B
The strength of the field, measured in tesla (T) = N·s/(C·m) = Wb/m2. Earth’s field ≈ 50 µT; a fridge magnet ≈ 5 mT; an MRI scanner 1.5–3 T; the strongest laboratory fields ≈ 45 T.

3.2 · Magnetic field lines and bar magnets

Field-line rules for magnetism

  • Outside the magnet, lines run from the north pole to the south pole.
  • Lines are closed loops — they continue through the magnet from S to N. This is the difference from electric field lines, which start and stop on charges.
  • Lines never cross; the field has one direction at each point.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. The field is most intense at the poles, where the lines bunch together.
  • The tangent to a line is the direction a free north pole (or a compass needle’s north end) would point.
A naming trap

The “north pole” of a compass needle points toward the geographic North Pole — which means the Earth’s magnetic pole near geographic north is actually a south magnetic pole. Opposite poles attract. Geomagnetic north is currently in the Canadian Arctic and drifts about 40 km per year.

3.3 · Force on a moving charge

A charge moving through a magnetic field experiences a force perpendicular to both its velocity and the field. This is the magnetic part of the Lorentz force:

F = q v × B |F| = |q| v B sinθ θ = the angle between v and B · F in newtons, v in m/s, B in tesla
  • θ = 0° or 180° (v parallel to B) → F = 0. The charge is unaffected.
  • θ = 90° (v perpendicular to B) → F = |q|vB, the maximum.
  • A stationary charge feels no magnetic force at all, however strong the field.
  • Reversing the sign of q or the direction of B reverses the force.
The right-hand rule (for a positive charge)

Flat right hand: fingers point along v, curl them toward B; your extended thumb points along F. Equivalent version: index finger = v, middle finger = B, thumb = F, all mutually perpendicular.

For a negative charge (an electron), find the direction with your right hand and then reverse it — or just use your left hand.

Notation for fields perpendicular to the page

(a dot) = the vector comes out of the page toward you — the tip of an arrow. (a cross) = the vector goes into the page — the feathers of an arrow seen from behind.

Circular motion

Because F is always perpendicular to v, the magnetic force acts as a centripetal force. It changes the direction of the velocity but never its magnitude:

|q|vB = mv2/r r = mv / (|q|B) T = 2πm / (|q|B) the period does not depend on speed — faster particles simply travel a wider circle in the same time
Worked example

A proton enters a 0.50 T field at 2.0×105 m/s, perpendicular to the field. Find the force, radius and period.

F = qvB = (1.602×10−19)(2.0×105)(0.50) = 1.60×10−14 N
r = mv/(qB) = (1.673×10−27)(2.0×105) / (1.602×10−19 × 0.50) = 3.35×10−22 / 8.01×10−20 = 4.2 mm
T = 2πm/(qB) = 2π(1.673×10−27)/(8.01×10−20) = 1.31×10−7 s (131 ns, i.e. f ≈ 7.6 MHz)

Replace the proton with an electron at the same speed and the radius shrinks by the mass ratio to about 2.3 µm — the electron curves the opposite way, and 1836 times more tightly.

Why no work is done

Power = F·v = |F||v|cos90° = 0. A magnetic field can never speed a charge up or slow it down; it can only steer it. That is why a cyclotron needs an electric field to add energy and a magnetic field to keep the beam circulating — and it is why the kinetic energy in the Lorentz simulator stays constant.

Force on a current-carrying wire (the motor effect)

A wire carries moving charges, so it too feels a force: F = BIL sinθ. This is the principle of the electric motor — a loop in a field experiences a couple that makes it turn. Fleming’s left-hand rule (thumb = motion, first finger = field, second finger = current) gives the same answer as the right-hand rule applied to the positive charge carriers.

Real world

Mass spectrometers separate ions by m/q: ions are accelerated, then bent in a known field, and r = mv/(qB) tells you the mass. This is how we measure isotopes and identify molecules.

The aurora. Charged particles from the solar wind spiral along Earth’s field lines and are funnelled toward the magnetic poles, where they excite oxygen (green, 557.7 nm) and nitrogen (red/blue). The same deflection shields the whole planet from the solar wind.

MRI scanners use 1.5–3 T fields to make proton spins precess; particle colliders use thousands of superconducting dipole magnets to bend beams around a 27 km ring; eddy-current brakes on trains and roller coasters use the same force with no friction and no wear.

3.5 · Magnetic flux

Flux measures how much magnetic field threads a loop. It depends on the field strength, the area of the loop, and the orientation between them:

Φ = B · A · cosθ general form: Φ = ∫ B · dA Φ in webers (Wb) = T·m2 · θ is the angle between B and the normal to the loop

A loop of area 0.02 m2 in a 0.5 T field has Φ = 0.01 Wb when the field is perpendicular to its plane, and zero when the field lies in the plane — even though the field is just as strong. Rotate the loop and the flux changes.

3.6 · Faraday’s law

EMF = − N · dΦ/dt N = number of turns · EMF in volts · Φ in webers · t in seconds

The induced EMF is proportional to the rate of change of flux linkage — not to the flux itself. A huge but constant flux induces nothing at all. There are exactly three ways to change Φ = BA cosθ:

  1. Change B — move a magnet toward or away from the coil, or vary the current in a nearby electromagnet.
  2. Change A — stretch, squash or slide the loop so the enclosed area changes.
  3. Change θ — rotate the coil in the field. This is what every generator does.
Worked example 1 — changing B

A 200-turn coil of area 0.010 m2 sits perpendicular to a field that rises from 0 to 0.5 T in 0.20 s.

dΦ/dt = A·dB/dt = 0.010 × (0.5/0.20) = 0.025 Wb/s
EMF = N·dΦ/dt = 200 × 0.025 = 5.0 V

Worked example 2 — rotating a coil (an AC generator)

A coil of N turns and area A rotates at angular frequency ω in a field B, so θ = ωt:

Φ = BA cos(ωt)  →  EMF = −N dΦ/dt = NBAω sin(ωt)
peak EMF = NBAω. With N = 100, B = 0.5 T, A = 0.02 m2, f = 50 Hz (ω = 314 rad/s):
peak = 100 × 0.5 × 0.02 × 314 = 314 V

That is a mains-sized voltage, produced by a hand-sized coil spinning 50 times a second. The output is sinusoidal and alternating — which is why the grid carries AC.

Motional EMF

A rod of length L sliding at speed v perpendicular to a field B has its free charges deflected by F = qvB until the electric field they set up balances the magnetic one. The result is a voltage across the rod:

EMF = B L va 0.20 m rod at 3 m/s in 0.5 T gives 0.30 V

If the rod completes a circuit of resistance R, the current is I = BLv/R and the magnetic force on that current is F = BIL = B2L2v/R, opposing the motion. The mechanical power you supply, Fv = B2L2v2/R, is exactly the electrical power I2R dissipated in the resistor — energy conservation is built into the equations.

3.7 · Lenz’s law and the minus sign

The negative sign in Faraday’s law is not a formality; it is a complete physical statement known as Lenz’s law:

Lenz’s law

The induced current flows in such a direction that its own magnetic field opposes the change in flux that produced it.

Note carefully: it opposes the change, not the flux itself.

Why Lenz’s law must be true

Suppose the induced current helped the change instead of opposing it. The magnet would accelerate, increasing the flux faster, driving a bigger current, which would pull the magnet harder still — energy from nowhere. Lenz’s law is conservation of energy applied to induction: the electrical energy dissipated as I2R heat is paid for by the mechanical work you do against the magnetic opposition. If you stop pushing, the current stops immediately.

The three-step recipe for finding a direction

1. Is the flux through the loop increasing or decreasing? 2. The induced field must oppose that change — so it points opposite to B if the flux is rising, and along B if it is falling. 3. Curl your right-hand fingers around the loop in the current direction; your thumb then points along the induced field. That fixes the current direction.

Real world

Generators rotate a coil in a field (change θ) — a 1 GW turbine is Faraday’s law at industrial scale. Transformers use a changing current in the primary to create a changing flux in an iron core, inducing a voltage in the secondary; the turns ratio sets the voltage ratio, which is why the grid can transmit at 400 kV and deliver at 230 V.

Induction hobs drive 20–50 kHz AC through a coil, inducing eddy currents that heat the pan directly — the glass stays cool. Wireless phone chargers are small transformers with an air gap. Eddy-current brakes slow trains and fairground drop towers with no contact and no wear. Guitar pickups have a magnet inside a coil; a vibrating steel string changes the flux and generates the signal. Airport security and metal detectors look for the eddy currents induced in metal objects.

3.9 · Check your understanding

An electron moves east at 106 m/s through a field of 0.2 T directed into the page. Find the force’s magnitude and direction.
3.2×10−14 N, directed south (down the page).
Magnitude: F = |q|vB sin90° = (1.602×10−19)(106)(0.2) = 3.2×10−14 N.
Direction: take east = +x̂, north = +ŷ, out of the page = +ẑ, so B = −ẑB. Then v × B = (v x̂) × (−B ẑ) = −vB(x̂ × ẑ) = −vB(−ŷ) = +ŷ = north for a positive charge. The electron carries −e, so the force reverses: south. Always evaluate the cross product first, then apply the sign of q.
A proton and an alpha particle enter the same field with the same speed. Which has the larger orbit radius?
The alpha particle, by a factor of about 2. From r = mv/(|q|B), with v and B fixed, r is proportional to m/|q|. For a proton m/|q| = mp/e; for an alpha particle (a helium nucleus, m ≈ 4mp, q = +2e) m/|q| ≈ 4mp/2e = 2mp/e. Both curve the same way because both are positive — they simply land on different radii. Measuring that radius is exactly how a mass spectrometer identifies an ion.
Can a magnetic field change a particle’s kinetic energy?
No. F is always perpendicular to v, so F·v = 0 and no work is done. Speed and kinetic energy stay constant; only the direction changes. To change the energy you need an electric field.
Why do field lines never cross, and why must magnetic lines close on themselves?
If two lines crossed, B would have two directions at one point, which is impossible for a vector field. Magnetic lines close because ∇·B = 0 — there are no magnetic monopoles to act as sources or sinks. Electric field lines, by contrast, begin and end on charges.
A proton and an electron move with the same velocity through the same field. Compare their forces.
Equal in magnitude, opposite in direction — because |q| is the same (e) for both. Their accelerations, though, differ by the mass ratio 1836: the electron is deflected far more sharply, which is why the aurora and CRT beams are dominated by electron motion.
A 500-turn coil of area 4.0×10−3 m2 is perpendicular to a field that falls from 0.80 T to 0.20 T in 0.10 s. Find the induced EMF.
12 V. dΦ/dt = A·dB/dt = 4.0×10−3 × (0.60/0.10) = 0.024 Wb/s. EMF = N·dΦ/dt = 500 × 0.024 = 12 V.
A magnet is held stationary inside a coil connected to a galvanometer. What does the meter read, and why?
Zero. The flux is large but constant, so dΦ/dt = 0 and EMF = −N·dΦ/dt = 0. Induction requires change. This is why generators must turn and why transformers only work on AC.
You push a magnet into a coil twice as fast. What happens to the EMF, the current and the energy dissipated?
EMF doubles (dΦ/dt doubles), current doubles (I = EMF/R), and power quadruples (P = EMF2/R). The energy dissipated per pass doubles: the pass takes half as long but burns four times the power (×4 × ½ = ×2) — equivalently, the magnetic drag force doubles while you push through the same distance. (The total charge per pass is unchanged: Q = ΔΦ/R.) That doubled effort is exactly Lenz’s law demanding payment.
Why does the EMF trace cross zero as the magnet passes through the centre of the coil?
At the centre the flux is at its maximum, and at a maximum the derivative is zero. The flux stops rising and starts falling, so dΦ/dt — and therefore the EMF — passes through zero and reverses sign. The trace has two opposite peaks, one on the way in and one on the way out.
A copper ring is held above a solenoid. When the solenoid’s current is switched on, the ring jumps upward. Explain.
The rising current increases the flux through the ring, inducing a current in it. By Lenz’s law that current opposes the increase, so the ring’s field repels the solenoid’s field and the ring is thrown upward. If the ring has a slit in it, no current can flow and it does not jump — a classic lecture demonstration.
Does the resistance of the coil change the induced EMF? Does it change the induced current?
No to the first, yes to the second. EMF = −N·dΦ/dt depends only on the geometry and the motion. But I = EMF/R, so a lower resistance gives a larger current, more heating and a stronger braking force — which is why eddy-current brakes use thick copper rather than steel.
