Waves & sound

Waves carry energy without carrying material. This topic covers the vocabulary, the master equation v = fλ, and sound as a wave you can actually hear.

A wave is a disturbance that travels, carrying energy without carrying matter. Ripples, sound, seismic tremors and light are all the same idea wearing different costumes — and one equation, v = fλ, ties them together.

Learning objectives

  • Define amplitude, wavelength, frequency and period, with their units.
  • Apply v = fλ and T = 1/f to any periodic wave.
  • Distinguish transverse from longitudinal waves, with examples.
  • Relate pitch to frequency and loudness to amplitude for sound.
  • Explain why wave speed belongs to the medium, not the source.

4.1 · Describing a wave

Four numbers describe any wave: how tall it is (amplitude), the length of one cycle (wavelength), how many cycles per second (frequency), and the seconds per cycle (period).

Amplitude A — the biggest displacement
How far the medium swings from rest, in metres (or the field’s strength, for light). Amplitude carries the energy: double A and a wave carries four times the energy.
Wavelength λ — one complete cycle, in space
Crest to crest (or compression to compression), in metres.
Frequency f — cycles per second
In hertz (Hz). Set by the source — it does not change when the wave enters a new medium.
Period T — seconds per cycle
T = 1/f. One crest passes every T seconds.
v = f · λ wave speed in m/s — each second, f wavelengths of length λ go past

The wave speed v is a property of the medium: sound flies through steel (~5100 m/s) fifteen times faster than through air (343 m/s); waves on a tighter string run faster. Since f is fixed by the source, entering a new medium forces λ to adapt: λ = v/f.

Worked example

Ocean swell arrives with crests every 8 s, spaced 12 m apart. How fast is it moving?

f = 1/T = 0.125 Hz · v = fλ = 0.125 × 12 = 1.5 m/s — a slow walking pace, which is why swells feel so unhurried.

4.2 · Transverse vs longitudinal

Waves either wiggle side-to-side across their travel (transverse, like a rope) or squeeze-and-stretch along it (longitudinal, like sound).

Common misconceptions

1. “The medium travels with the wave.” It does not — a floating duck bobs in place while the swell passes. Only the disturbance (and its energy) moves. Turn on “medium particles” in the simulator and watch a single dot: up and down, never along.

2. “Higher frequency means faster wave.” Only if the medium lets it. In air, a 100 Hz bass note and a 10 kHz whistle travel at the same 343 m/s — the whistle just has a 100× shorter wavelength.

3. “Sound is transverse because you can draw it as a wiggly line.” Oscilloscope traces plot pressure against time — they are graphs, not pictures of the air. In the air itself, sound is compressions marching along the direction of travel.

4.4 · Sound

Sound is a squeezing-and-stretching wave in air. Higher frequency means higher pitch; bigger amplitude means louder.

Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave: a loudspeaker cone punches compressions into the air, and your eardrum reads them back. It needs a medium — ring a bell under a vacuum jar and the hammer keeps moving in silence.

  • Pitch is frequency. Human hearing spans about 20 Hz – 20 kHz; below that is infrasound (elephants), above it ultrasound (bats, medical imaging). Concert pitch A4 = 440 Hz; every octave doubles f.
  • Loudness is amplitude (physically, intensity ∝ A²), measured on the logarithmic decibel scale: +10 dB ≈ twice as loud to the ear.
  • Speed belongs to the medium: 343 m/s in air at 20 °C, 1480 m/s in water, ~5100 m/s in steel. Warm air carries sound slightly faster — v ≈ 331 + 0.6·T(°C).
Worked examples

Wavelength of concert A. λ = v/f = 343/440 = 0.78 m — about the width of a doorway, which is why bass (λ ≈ 3 m at 110 Hz) leaks through walls and treble does not.

How far is that storm? Flash, then thunder 6 s later: d = v·t = 343 × 6 ≈ 2 km. (The rule of thumb: 3 s ≈ 1 km.)

Real world — the Doppler effect

When a source moves, it chases its own wavefronts: in front, crests are squeezed together (higher f, higher pitch); behind, they are stretched (lower pitch). That is the nee-oww of a passing ambulance. The same shift, in light, is how we know the universe is expanding — distant galaxies are redshifted, their light waves stretched by recession.

4.6 · Check your understanding

A wave has f = 10 Hz and λ = 0.5 m. Find v and T.
v = fλ = 10 × 0.5 = 5 m/s; T = 1/f = 0.1 s.
Sound enters water, where it travels about 4.3× faster than in air. What happens to f and λ?
f is unchanged (the source still vibrates at the same rate — crests cannot pile up at the boundary), so λ must grow 4.3×: λ = v/f. Faster medium, longer wavelength, same pitch.
Which is longitudinal: (a) a guitar string’s wave, (b) the sound the string makes, (c) light from the stage lamps?
Only (b). The string vibrates perpendicular to its length (transverse) and light is transverse too — but the pressure wave the string launches into the air is compressions along the direction of travel: longitudinal.
You see lightning and hear the thunder 9 s later. How far away was the strike?
Light is essentially instantaneous; sound took 9 s: d = 343 × 9 ≈ 3.1 km.
On the tone player, doubling f from 220 to 440 Hz changes what you hear how? And what happens to λ in air?
The pitch rises exactly one octave (220 Hz is A3, 440 Hz is A4 — the same note name). Since v stays 343 m/s, λ halves: from 1.56 m to 0.78 m.
